Executive Summary

Analysis

October's defining shift is that the decisive events were not breaches of a victim's own system. Denmark's CPR register lost roughly 8.8 million people's data - about 80 per cent of it - through abuse of a private company's legitimate access; the Oracle Health/Cerner count accumulated past 19 million through state notifications rather than a company disclosure; Frontline Education, ETS and a Danish university's identity system each became the route into somebody else's students and staff; and ConnectWise turned a backup service into a path across more than 170 Microsoft 365 tenants. The largest victim counts of the month are the ones where the party carrying the risk was not the party holding the data.

The second shift is that AI-assisted operations became the lead item in a sector rather than a research curiosity. A China-linked actor used an AI pentesting suite and large-language-model agents to breach South Korean financial firms and expose at least 68,000 people, Microsoft attributed the ConnectWise supply-chain campaign to a China-state actor acting through AI agents, and one operator used autonomous agents against more than 1,200 sites. Set against that, the defender's side of the ledger was a record Patch Tuesday - 1,413 flaws and three zero-days - and a three-zero-day chain on a VPN appliance where only two had been patched.

The third is that the layers everything else trusts were the ones attacked. Three Ivanti Connect Secure zero-days chained for root and Active Directory credentials; eMudhra's intermediate certificate-authority keys compromised, forcing emergency browser and Go blocks and ending its TLS business; credential stealers shipped inside real PyPI wheels and tens of thousands of GitHub repositories; and a compromised remote-support platform, an internet-facing workplace-management platform and a run of internet-reachable building controllers. Attribution kept pace with neither: state campaigns were named faster than they were disrupted, and the enforcement action that followed - US$25 billion against Meta in New Mexico, US$1.2 billion against Bank of America - was financial rather than structural.

Watch-list

Next period

Forward indicators for the coming period. Each states what would confirm the direction of travel.

1

Agentic operations at criminal and state scale

An AI pentesting suite and large-language-model agents were used against multiple banks in one campaign, and a China-state actor ran a supply-chain operation through AI agents. Cost per target has fallen, so controls that assume human-paced probing will under-perform.

Indicator: Repeat targeting of banks or SaaS providers at machine cadence, sole-actor campaigns producing multi-victim scope, and incident reports describing automated recon followed by manual escalation.

2

Management-plane zero-days with an incomplete patch set

Three Ivanti Connect Secure zero-days were chained with only two patched, and the same window carried exploited flaws in FortiGate, PAN-OS, Cisco Secure Email Gateway and Arista VeloCloud. Appliances that grant estate-wide authority are being worked faster than vendors can patch them.

Indicator: Vendor advisories confirming exploitation while a full patch set is unavailable, and mitigation advice that reduces capability rather than removing the flaw.

3

Certificate-authority and package-registry integrity

eMudhra's intermediate CA keys were compromised and it withdrew from the TLS business, while credential stealers shipped in genuine PyPI wheels and tens of thousands of GitHub repositories. Integrity failures at this layer are measured in the whole ecosystem, not the victim.

Indicator: Emergency browser or language-runtime blocks, any CA withdrawal or distrust event, and registries publishing incidence data on takedowns rather than only advisories.

4

National registers reached through a vendor's access

Denmark's CPR lost about 80 per cent of its register data through a private company's abused legitimate access, Arizona courts lost 1.3 million records, and a Pentagon file-sharing flaw exposed 3 million people's details. Entitlement hygiene is the control under test.

Indicator: Disclosures whose stated mechanism is delegated or vendor access rather than exploitation, and government registers with broad third-party integration.

5

Set-point tampering against building and cold-chain systems

The French supermarket refrigeration attack manipulated physical set-points across hundreds of stores, Loxone and Eptura's Archibus added workplace and building-automation platforms to the list, and specialists expect more. Control systems are internet-reachable and owned by someone other than the risk carrier.

Indicator: Further set-point or physical-process events in retail, health or food logistics, and disclosures naming building-automation or refrigeration vendors rather than their customers.

Healthcare

Providers, payers, medical-device makers and health software suppliers.

1

Oracle Health/Cerner breach figure approaches 20 million as state disclosures accumulate

Reporting relayed by the Texas Attorney General indicates that a 2025 breach of protected health information on Oracle Health's legacy Cerner servers may have compromised the records of almost 20 million individuals — a figure not independently verified and never publicly totalled by Oracle Health, which has not said how many Cerner clients or individuals were affected. Where state attorneys general publish numbers, the count runs to 2,992,244 in Texas, 1,978,661 in Oregon, 283,903 in South Carolina and 69,238 in Washington, while the HHS Office for Civil Rights portal still carries a placeholder total of 501 and is expected to be updated after the Texas AG was given a revised figure on 2 October. The breach notice states an unauthorised individual first gained access to legacy Cerner servers as early as 22 January 2025, identified by Oracle Health on 7 March 2025, and that compromised data includes names, Social Security numbers, diagnoses, treatment information, medications, test results, medical images, record numbers and physician names. The gap between the OCR placeholder and the state disclosures is itself a governance story about breach-reporting latency. Verification: Verified

2

Central Ohio Primary Care Physicians breach affects 1.4 million patients

Central Ohio Primary Care Physicians (COPC), one of the largest independent physician-owned primary care groups in the United States with more than 80 locations across Central Ohio, has disclosed a breach affecting approximately 1.4 million current and former patients after a voice-phishing (vishing) attack in August 2026 yielded third-party access to internal systems. The unauthorised access was detected and blocked on 25 August 2026, but the investigation confirmed that files were exfiltrated containing patient names, addresses, Social Security numbers, financial information, health information and other identifiers, covering patients past and present. Notification letters are being mailed with complimentary credit monitoring, identity-theft protection and dark-web monitoring, and the group says it has found no misuse of the data to date. No threat group was named in the breach notices, but the HIPAA Journal reports the incident appears to be the work of the Chaos ransomware group, which listed the practice on its dark-web leak site and threatened to publish the stolen data before the listing was withdrawn. It is one of the largest US patient-data breaches disclosed this year and shows vishing remains an effective first-stage route into healthcare networks.

3

Biosensor firm iRhythm says data of at least 360,000 people was breached in June cyberattack

Medical-device maker iRhythm, best known for its Zio Patch cardiac monitor, began filing breach notices in multiple US states this week, stating that the personal information of at least 360,000 people was stolen during a June cyberattack. State letters put 298,647 affected individuals in Texas and 69,526 in South Carolina, with a California filing also made; the company declined to give a full national total. iRhythm said hackers gained access to unidentified third-party-hosted business applications between 3 and 8 June through a social engineering attack and downloaded information including names, addresses, phone numbers, patient account numbers, device serial numbers, insurance numbers, dates of service and dates of birth. The company said its June SEC 8-K disclosure confirmed data was exfiltrated and the attackers demanded payment, but stressed that no clinical systems or medical devices were affected and that it had no evidence of identity theft. No group has publicly claimed credit. Verification: Verified

4

Privacy Commissioner closes Manage My Health inquiry with final report critical of data retention and digital-health governance

New Zealand Privacy Commissioner Michael Webster released the second and final report into the December 2025 Manage My Health (MMH) breach, formally closing the section-17 inquiry. Around 91 per cent of affected patients were Northland hospital patients; the breach compromised more than 400,000 Health NZ documents and over 22,000 patient-uploaded files. The phase-two report found MMH retained contact details and non-activated accounts far too long — its 24-month dormant-account process should be reduced to deletion after three months — and that many patients were unaware they had portal accounts at all. It warned the "pace of government digitisation was a real cause for concern" and that the health sector was not ready for a "big digital one-stop shop". Phase one (May 2026) found MMH and Health NZ breached Rule 5 (reasonable security safeguards), with compliance notices issued in September. Health NZ said the phase-two report identifies no new privacy breaches.

5

Vendor vulnerability leaves 341,000 Blueberry Pediatrics patients exposed across two separate breaches

Blueberry Pediatrics disclosed that a vulnerability in analytics tool Metabase, exploited against healthcare technology vendor Photon, led to two separate breaches of patient data affecting roughly 341,000 individuals (340,915 notified to the Oregon Attorney General; Texas received two reports covering 60,581 and 7,249 residents). Blueberry's data included names, dates of birth, health plan names and policy numbers, medical records, dates of service, medical codes and charges; its own patients' contact details, member IDs and medication information were also exposed. Photos and videos from patients were stored separately and not compromised, and no financial data or Social Security numbers were involved. Blueberry applied the Metabase patch within hours, but a threat actor already had access, and Photon advised that a different actor later also obtained a copy of patient data. The incident is not yet listed on the HHS OCR breach portal.

Source: HIPAA Journal · Tier 2/4 — High · 2026-10-28 · Impact 20 ·
6

New BYOD ransomware gang claims attack on University of New Mexico hospital network

The University of New Mexico Health System diverted ambulances to hospitals in Albuquerque and Santa Fe for about two hours on Monday 26 October and suspended outpatient surgeries and appointments for the day after a cyberattack hit its network. The organisation, which operates New Mexico's only burn unit, children's hospital and Level 1 trauma centre, contained the incident and notified federal law enforcement; staff were told to unplug desktops and disconnect mobile devices from hospital Wi-Fi. Services largely resumed late Monday, but classes across the School of Medicine, College of Nursing, College of Pharmacy and College of Population Health were cancelled Tuesday. A ransomware gang calling itself BYOD — which emerged last month with only a handful of known victims — claimed responsibility, alleging it compromised 36,000 machines across the main campus and hospital and stole data on students, patients and employees. The Health System, citing an active law-enforcement investigation, has not confirmed data theft or whether ransomware was deployed. It is the second major attack on a large US state hospital this year after University of Mississippi Medical Center's weeks-long disruption in March.

DOJ charges a ransomware-recovery CEO with secretly paying attackers while overbilling victims

The US Justice Department unsealed charges on 8 October against Zohar Pinhasi, chief executive of the ransomware-recovery firm MonsterCloud, alleging he defrauded clients by secretly negotiating and paying ransom demands while billing them for services he presented as delivering decryption. Japan confirms arrest of suspected Qilin ransomware operative and extradition to Germany

Japan's National Police Agency confirmed on 8 October the arrest and extradition to Germany of a 28-year-old Russian national alleged to be involved in the Qilin ransomware gang, on a German arrest warrant over a ransomware attack on a German company. 4

23andMe successor to pay California US$6 million over genetic-data breach

California has settled its claims against 23andMe — now owned by the non-profit TTAM Research Institute — for US$6 million, resolving alleged violations of state law over the 2023 credential-stuffing breach that exposed the personal and genetic data of about 6.9 million people, including 855,000 Californians. Attorney General Rob Bonta's office opted out of the US$18 million multistate settlement agreed in July with 42 other attorneys general; under the new deal the state drops its appeals of a bankruptcy-court ruling that barred it from suing TTAM over the breach after the company's US$305 million sale. Customers retain the right to demand deletion of their personal and genetic data. 23andMe separately paid US$46.75 million to settle consumer class actions, approved in July 2026. Why it matters: the settlement closes the costliest privacy chapter of the breach while establishing that a post-bankruptcy asset sale does not extinguish state genetic-privacy enforcement — and it preserves consumer deletion rights over data now held by a different legal entity.

New Mexico court orders Meta to pay US$25bn over 43 million privacy violations

A New Mexico state district court ordered Meta on 21 October to pay US$25bn in penalties after finding more than 43 million violations of the state's unfair practices law, in litigation that grew out of the 2018 Cambridge Analytica revelations. Judge Francis J. Mathew wrote that Facebook promised users they controlled their data and that "this was false", holding the company's privacy statements misleading. The case was filed in 2021 after Cambridge Analytica obtained data on roughly 87 million profiles through a personality quiz app that also harvested participants' friends' data. Meta's chief legal officer C.J. Mahoney said the company will appeal. The penalty is by far the largest privacy sanction recorded against the company: it dwarfs the US$725m class-action settlement of 2022 and the US$5bn Federal Trade Commission penalty of 2019, and lands alongside an US$18bn multi-state child-safety settlement agreed in August. Defence

6 events

Defence-industrial base, state-sponsored espionage and military-adjacent targeting.

1

China-linked Armory Castle exploited a BeyondTrust Remote Support zero-day (CVE-2026-102764)

BeyondTrust, Mandiant and Google Threat Intelligence Group disclosed on 15–16 October a zero-day in BeyondTrust Remote Support appliances, tracked as CVE-2026-102764, exploited as early as 29 August 2026. The actor — Mandiant's ARMORY CASTLE, also tracked as UNC5221 — used the flaw to escalate privileges on the appliance, move laterally and establish a foothold whose files survived reboots and configuration resets, and it obtained every credential the appliance stored. Mandiant reported custom malware it calls Sawfly and Bareclaw and said the group "leveraged a deep understanding of BeyondTrust appliance internals". BeyondTrust described ARMORY CASTLE as a Chinese state-sponsored espionage operation focused on long-term intelligence collection, with documented targets including legal services, SaaS providers, business-process outsourcers, technology companies and government. Google warned the actor has previously conducted widespread opportunistic exploitation immediately after edge-device disclosures and said similar attempts against this flaw are possible. A patch and mitigations are available; Mandiant advised that any vulnerable appliance reachable from the internet should be treated as potentially compromised until reviewed. CISA added CVE-2026-102764 to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 16 October. Verification: Verified

Source: The Record · Tier 1/4 — Very High · 2026-10-16 · Impact 56 · Confirmed
2

ESET: Russia-aligned UAC-0099 steadily upgrades its MATCHBOIL downloader against Ukrainian government and industry

ESET published research on 8 October documenting the MATCHBOIL downloader used by UAC-0099, a group it assesses with medium confidence to be aligned with Russian interests, across versions compiled or observed between April 2024 and April 2026 — each more evasive than the last. University of Illinois Chicago was hit by a ransomware attack on its College of Medicine; the Booba gang claimed 344 GB of stolen data

The University of Illinois Chicago (UIC), the largest university in Chicago with more than 35,000 students, confirmed a ransomware attack that limited access to some systems at its College of Medicine. A spokesperson told The Record that attackers "were able to steal some information held on the college's servers," that "some College of Medicine systems were temporarily unavailable" and have since been restored, and that investigations are under way to determine whether "any personal, research or academic information was compromised." The university says its main network was not affected, there was no impact on patient care at UI Health, and the incident was reported to law enforcement, with notification planned for anyone whose information was stolen. The attack was claimed last week by the Booba ransomware gang, which said it had stolen 344 GB of data; researchers assess Booba to be a rebrand of the Frag ransomware based on leak-site style and negotiation flow. Why it matters: ransomware at a medical school sits on the healthcare/education boundary, where a single compromise can touch patient, student and research data at once — and the Booba rebrand signals continued churn in ransomware branding that operators must track. 2

The Technical University of Denmark Disclosed a Breach of Up to 200,000 People's Data Via Its Identity and Access Management System

The Technical University of Denmark (DTU) disclosed on Friday 2 October that attackers used compromised credentials to log into DTUBasen, its identity and access management (IAM) system, and downloaded a large amount of data potentially covering up to 200,000 people — nearly 40,000 active users and around 160,000 former users. DTU said it cannot determine precisely what information was downloaded or how many people were affected, but acknowledged the dataset includes Danish civil registration numbers (CPR), full names, home addresses, profile pictures, work email addresses and job titles for current users, plus next-of-kin names, relationships and phone numbers where supplied. University Director Bjarke Bak Christensen called it "a serious attack on DTU" and said priority was establishing the extent, limiting consequences and notifying those affected. DTU warned the exposed CPR numbers could be used for identity fraud and targeted phishing. It is a first-party disclosure of a breach of a national technical university's central identity system, and a reminder of the outsized value of IAM databases to attackers. Verification: Verified

3

California warns all public school students after ETS contractor breach

California's Department of Education (CDE) has begun notifying families that assessment contractor Educational Testing Service (ETS) suffered a months-long breach in which hackers copied sensitive student records through a secure file transfer protocol site between 22 May and 18 September 2026. The data covers essentially every child enrolled in California public schools over the past three years — names, dates of birth, residential addresses, schools, districts and grade levels, statewide and local student identifiers, gender, race or ethnicity, primary language and country of origin, English-learner status, disability and Section 504 status, testing accommodations, Title I and McKinney-Vento eligibility, and assessment registration, participation and results — plus students who left a California school since 2006 without a confirmed transfer. ETS, which runs the CAASPP and ELPAC exams for a system that enrolled more than 5.7 million students in the 2025 school year, discovered the breach on 25 September, disabled the abused account, took the site offline and rebuilt the platform, and has paused normal enrolment data processing and some testing while systems are assessed. No hacking group had claimed responsibility as of 20 October. It is the broadest exposure of K-12 pupil data disclosed this year.

Frontline Education Is Notifying School Districts That a Third-Party Software Flaw Led to Unauthorised Access and Theft of Employee Data Including Social Security Numbers

Edtech provider Frontline Education, which supplies administration and workforce-management software to US school districts, is notifying districts of a data breach after attackers exploited a vulnerability in a third-party application to gain unauthorised access to its systems and steal employee information. A notification to one impacted district says the company's security team identified the third-party vulnerability on 14 August 2026, allowing unauthorised access to part of the environment; Frontline says it investigated with an independent cybersecurity firm, remediated the flaw, engaged law enforcement and reinforced system security. It has not disclosed which third-party application was involved or when the first unauthorised access occurred. For the notification reported by BleepingComputer on 2 October, all employees at the affected district were impacted, with exposed data including Social Security numbers, email addresses and physical addresses. School IT administrators on the K12SysAdmin subreddit reported that districts began receiving similar notifications on 1 October, and multiple administrators independently confirmed the notifications are legitimate. 5

Springfield Public Schools confirms Social Security numbers dating back decades were stolen in September cyberattack

Springfield Public Schools in Massachusetts said on 9 October that the cyberattack first detected around 4 September — which took computers, email, phones and vital records offline and forced a week of cancelled classes — compromised the Social Security numbers of employees hired before 2008, former staff who worked in 2009 or earlier, volunteers who assisted in 2001 or earlier, and long-serving independent contractors. Assistant Superintendent Jose Escribano said SSNs of staff hired after 2008 are believed to be protected and students should not be affected because the schools do not routinely record their numbers; investigators are still working out which former-employee and volunteer records included SSNs. Identity-theft and credit-monitoring services through IDX, initially offered to current staff, have been extended to former employees and volunteers. Of more than 20,000 staff and student laptops requiring secure rebuilds, about 65 per cent of staff machines are restored. The FBI, Massachusetts State Police and local police are investigating; officials describe the attackers only as a sophisticated global organisation, and no group claim or ransom detail is public. Verification: Reported

6

University of Copenhagen confirms breach of test environment exposing 525,000 records

Denmark's University of Copenhagen has confirmed hackers gained access to one of its test environments, exposing personal data belonging to 525,000 people — full names, national identity (CPR) numbers, phone numbers and email addresses. The university detected the intrusion on 20 October and disclosed it on 23 October, then updated on 25 October that the PIN codes of a further 20,000 staff and students had also been exposed, prompting it to restrict physical access to some critical campus locations as a precaution. The institution, Denmark's oldest, founded in 1479, has approximately 36,800 students and 10,700 employees across six faculties; it is still investigating the incident's full scope. The exposed records raise the risk of targeted phishing and social-engineering campaigns against students and staff, the university warned. The disclosure is first-party and one of the largest education-sector breaches reported in Europe in the past year, on a scale comparable to the 2023 MOVEit-linked attacks on UK universities.

1

Building-automation maker Loxone loses CRM and customer data as a group called Samurai defaces its site

Austrian building-automation manufacturer Loxone reported on 19 October that intruders reached its internal servers over the weekend, copying data and corrupting its internal CRM and the website's database. Loxone said the data taken centres on CRM records — contact details for partner businesses, chiefly electrical installation firms — plus support requests and applicant data, and described some of it, including corporate mailboxes and payslips, as outdated. Sample data circulating from the breach reportedly includes Microsoft 365 account details, SSO login information and a register of 300,000 Miniserver serial numbers with project names. A group calling itself Samurai defaced Loxone's website and claims roughly 200 GB was copied; Loxone declined to comment on a ransom demand or on the attack path, though it said its IT teams identified and closed the exploited route, and chief executive Florian Wöss told ORF the entry came through one of its web servers. Loxone says customer installations are unaffected because credentials sit locally on the Miniserver and because Miniserver, Remote Connect and Exosphere were untouched, and that it has notified authorities and engaged external forensics. Why it matters: compromising a controller vendor can hand an attacker a map of a widely deployed installed base even while the buildings themselves keep running.

2

Johnson Controls Kantech Entrapass uses a hard-coded cryptographic key, enabling a padding-oracle attack

CVE-2026-71456, published on 26 October, describes a hard-coded cryptographic key in Johnson Controls' Kantech Entrapass, the software used to manage physical access control systems, including door controllers, badges and site permissions. The hard-coded key allows a padding-oracle cryptographic attack, which in practice gives an attacker a route to decrypt or forge protected data without knowing the key. The flaw affects Entrapass versions before 9.20. Because Entrapass is the management layer for physical access rather than a data-plane product, the material risk is to the integrity of access records and, depending on deployment, to the credentials that door controllers trust — a building- or estate-level issue rather than a single-site one. Operators should confirm whether their access controllers accept software updates independently of the Entrapass server, since patching the server alone does not necessarily re-key hardware that has already been issued a credential. Rated 8.5 on CVSS 4.0. Verification: Verified

Rapid7 discloses critical unauthenticated RCE chain in Eptura Archibus workplace platform

Rapid7 has published three chained vulnerabilities in Eptura Archibus, the workplace and real-estate management platform that tracks assets, maintenance work orders, leases and desk or room reservations, and which Eptura says was used by more than 16,000 companies and 40% of the Fortune 500 as of 2023. CVE-2026-107186 (CVSS 8.6) abuses Archibus Web Central's Java Direct Web Remoting endpoint: the pre-authentication isPasswordUppercase method loads a core user session onto the thread, and a second batched call to runWorkflowRule then runs workflow rules under that session with no credentials. Chained with it, CVE-2026-107187 (CVSS 8.5) reads arbitrary database tables through the AbCommonResources-getDataRecords workflow — Rapid7 extracted user names and passwords from the afm_users table — while CVE-2026-107188 (CVSS 9.6, critical) allows JSP/JSPX uploads via export reports, yielding command execution as NT AUTHORITY\Local Service. Rapid7 found the flaws through continuous red teaming and says all three are fixed, and CISA's SSVC record lists no known exploitation. Why it matters: facility and property teams run Archibus inside corporate networks, so an internet-facing instance is a direct path to stored credentials and code execution on a building-management platform.

Methodology & Caveats

Provenance

Collection window: 1–22 October 2026 (partial-month collection, live-synced daily). Compiled 23 October 2026. 63 distinct events are reported in full, representing 63 digest entries after folding repeat coverage of the same event, with a further 88 lower-impact items listed in short form.

Collection: Built from the Cyber Digest SQLite database (cyber-digest.db) for the stated collection window, topped up by targeted web collection where a sector did not yield enough distinct events (Construction & Property). Every entry carries a deep link to the reporting it is drawn from; entries are written from the source article, not from headline aggregation.

Ranking: Events are ranked by an impact score (0-100), not by source reliability. The score adds fixed points for confirmed data loss, records affected, operational disruption, active exploitation, criticality of the victim environment, third-party delivery, state attribution and Australian/NZ nexus. Source reliability is recorded separately as a tier and never raises a score. The practical effect is that a confirmed breach of any scale outranks an advisory with no known victim, and a well-sourced advisory cannot top a sector on the strength of its publisher alone.

Evidence grading: Each entry carries one evidence grade: Confirmed (victim, regulator or vendor has stated the facts), Probable (reported breach with unresolved scope or attribution), Reported (single-source research, vendor or press reporting), Claimed (an extortion or hacktivist group's assertion, unverified), Thwarted (an official statement that an attack was blocked before compromise). Where only a claimed figure exists, the claim is used and the entry says so.

How this is produced: Cyber Digest is produced by an automated collection and drafting pipeline with editorial review. Sources are gathered and summarised by software, then checked, scored and edited before publication. Every entry links to its original source so claims can be checked independently; nothing in this report should be relied on without that check.

Limitations

  • The incident set is bounded by what the collection window surfaced and by the capacity of each sector section. It is a curated sample of notable events, not a census; absence of an incident here is not evidence it did not occur.
  • This is a partial-month collection to 22 October 2026 and will be superseded by the month-end edition; counts and sector leads can move before the window closes.
  • Victim counts are usually the figures reported at the time of writing and change as investigations complete. Claimed record counts are marked as claims and should not be read as verified.
  • Attribution is stated at the confidence the source supports. Where reporting offers a state nexus at medium confidence, the entry says so and the actor roll-up carries the same caveat rather than upgrading it.
  • Collection is weighted to English-language, publicly reported incidents, which over-represents US regulatory activity and under-represents incidents in jurisdictions with weaker disclosure obligations.
  • Australian and New Zealand relevance is flagged per event; where it is asserted in a sector narrative it refers to that flagged nexus, not to a general applicability claim. Two Australian actions anchor the window: the OAIC's investigation of the HeyCyan smart-glasses app maker and the ACSC joint advisory on Chinese government-linked actors.
  • 46 of the 63 events reported in full are graded Confirmed; the remainder are probable, reported or claimed and are labelled as such.
