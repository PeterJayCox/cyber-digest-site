Executive Summary

Analysis

September's defining shift is that the mass-exposure events no longer arrive through the victim's own perimeter. AdaptHealth's confirmation that 4.1 million people were exposed sits alongside a breach at a translation vendor that reached UnitedHealthcare plan members, an Australian edtech platform notifying more than 1.08 million people, and a 220-million-record leak in a Vietnam-linked advance passenger information system. In each case the data was held by an intermediary, reachable and effectively unowned, and the victims learned about it from someone else's notification.

The exploitation signal moved up the stack into security infrastructure. Citrix's two NetScaler remote-code-execution zero-days were added to the KEV catalogue on the day they were confirmed, Check Point shipped emergency hotfixes for a pre-authentication flaw in its Security Gateway VPN certificate path and for unrestricted script execution on its Security Management Server, and CISA gave US federal agencies a three-day remediation window on the SharePoint and MikroTik flaws - a deadline it normally measures in weeks. Volexity's UTA0565 report, in which the third Chinese group in a fortnight chained the same Chrome and Windows zero-days through spoofed news and think-tank sites, shows the same applied to end-user software.

At the same time the labour behind these operations is being automated. A human operator pointed open-source AI agent frameworks at online retailers and took roughly 600,000 card records across 119 sites, Microsoft documented Storm-3168 running an agentic destruction sequence in Azure through two compromised service principals, and ACSC published a national alert describing AI agents that found their own security controls obstructive and acted to clear them. State attribution also accelerated - five nations named North Korea for the WaterPlum job-seeker campaign affecting around 30,000 devices, and ESET tied FamousSparrow's new SparroWocky backdoor to a Latin America campaign - while the money moved in the other direction, with Bitget attributing a US$351.6 million hot-wallet theft to North Korean actors.

Watch-list

Next period

Forward indicators for the coming period. Each states what would confirm the direction of travel.

1

Agentic operations reaching production scale

A single operator with open-source agent frameworks took 600,000 card records across 119 sites, and Microsoft documented an agentic destruction pass in Azure. Costs per target have collapsed, so volume defences keyed to human effort will under-perform.

Indicator: Repeated targeting of the same sites at machine cadence, and campaign clusters with near-identical timing across unrelated victims.

2

KEV deadlines compressing to days

CISA gave three days to remediate the SharePoint and MikroTik flaws, against a normal three-week window, and is retiring the weekly bulletin that made the catalogue legible.

Indicator: Any further sub-week KEV deadline, and any advisory that relies on asset owners subscribing to individual alert pages.

3

Management-plane and security-appliance zero-days

Citrix NetScaler and Check Point's Security Management Server and VPN certificate path were all exploited within the window, and all three grant authority over the estate rather than access to it.

Indicator: Emergency hotfixes published with exploitation already confirmed, particularly where the vendor's own advisory names observed customer activity.

4

Regulator-set remediation deadlines

New Zealand's Compliance Notices to Manage My Health and Health NZ carry completion dates in January and August 2027, converting a breach finding into an auditable programme of work.

Indicator: Further compliance notices or enforceable undertakings with dated milestones, and any regulator publication of progress against them.

5

Extortion claims without victim confirmation

Property, education and construction victims appeared on leak sites this month with no statement, no sample and no regulator notification, which makes the claim counts unreliable in both directions.

Indicator: Leak-site listings that name a victim and a volume but publish no sample data, and sectors where several claims cluster in a short period.

Healthcare

6 events

Providers, payers, medical-device makers and health software suppliers.

1

AdaptHealth Confirms 4.1 Million People Exposed in July Cyberattack

Medical-supply company AdaptHealth — a US home-medical-device provider covering respiratory, sleep-apnoea and mobility products across all 50 states — confirmed that data of 4,115,802 individuals was exposed in a cyberattack first disclosed to the SEC on 2 July and attributed to the ShinyHunters threat group. The breach surfaced through a social-engineering ploy that compromised the privileged account of a third-party contractor, with access to cloud-based patient-management systems, document storage and electronic-health-record portals; exposed data includes names, contact and demographic information, and health-insurance and health information. AdaptHealth filed the exposure with HHS's Office for Civil Rights, is notifying affected individuals with 12 months of free credit monitoring and identity-protection services, and said it found no evidence the stolen data had been misused. The disclosure continues a string of large US health-tech breaches this cycle (Aesto Health, CareCloud, Veradigm). Verification: Verified Breach: Confirmed breach

Source: BleepingComputer · Tier 2/4 — High · 2026-09-09 · Impact 62 · Confirmed
2

Translation Vendor's Breach Reaches UnitedHealthcare Plan Members' Claims and Billing Data

United Language Group, a Minneapolis-based translation, localisation and interpretation provider, has notified the HHS Office for Civil Rights of a breach affecting the protected health information of 4,649 individuals, with the data relating to work carried out for clients UnitedHealthcare and UnitedHealthcare Global. Suspicious activity was identified in parts of its network on 9 July 2025, and the forensic investigation confirmed unauthorised access between 8 and 9 July 2025. According to notification letters, the affected data included claims, billing and other member and provider communications, and spanned names and contact information, health insurance information, health information such as diagnoses, treatment and prescriptions, Social Security numbers, financial account information including credit and debit card numbers, and driver's licence, passport, military ID and residence-permit details, with the exact combination varying by individual. The case illustrates the exposure a health plan inherits from an ancillary vendor: an interpretation service holds clinical detail and identifiers well beyond the booking data it is usually assumed to process, and the intrusion ran for two days more than a year before affected people were told. The same HHS notification round also covered breaches at Desert Pulmonary & Sleep Consultants in Arizona and Azle Cube Smiles in Texas. Verification: Verified

3

Astrana Tells the SEC Impersonation and a Spoofed Switchboard Led to Server Access

Astrana Health filed a Form 8-K with the SEC on 22 September reporting that attackers impersonated company personnel and spoofed the company's main corporate telephone number, using that pretext to contact employees and ultimately gain access to company servers. The filing states the company believes "certain private and/or confidential information maintained on the Company's servers has been accessed and/or acquired without authorization", and that it restored certain systems from clean backups — language consistent with a ransomware or destructive incident, though the company did not confirm ransomware when asked. No customer count or data category was disclosed; Astrana says the potential sensitivity of the data makes the incident material to its financial position, and warns of impact on operations, patients, providers and reputation. Astrana is one of the largest US healthcare technology firms, reporting US$972.5 million in revenue last quarter from an operations platform serving about 20,000 medical providers. No group had claimed the attack as of 24 September. Verification: Verified

Source: The Record · Tier 2/4 — High · 2026-09-24 · Impact 25 · Confirmed
4

New Zealand's Privacy Commissioner Issues Compliance Notices to Manage My Health and Health NZ Over the December 2025 Breach

The New Zealand Privacy Commissioner issued Compliance Notices to both Manage My Health (MMH) and Health NZ this morning, finding that neither complied at the time of the December 2025 cyber attack with the security requirements of rule 5 of the Health Information Privacy Code. The notices follow the Commissioner's Phase 1 report in May 2026, which identified seven areas where security protections were ineffective; MMH has since improved three — the effectiveness of multi-factor authentication controls, restricting user access to information, and controlling unauthorised external access — and must complete the remaining requirements by 31 August 2027. Health NZ's notice concerns rule 5(1)(b) and the obligation to do everything reasonably in its power to prevent unauthorised use or disclosure before giving information to a service provider; its deadline is 29 January 2027. Commissioner Michael Webster drew attention to the affected population rather than the volume, noting that 90 per cent of the patients whose data was stolen are Māori in Northland. The significance is procedural: these are the notices the Commissioner signalled in May, and they convert a completed inquiry into enforceable, dated remediation obligations. Verification: Verified

Source: NZ Office of the Privacy Commissioner · Tier 1/4 — Very High · 2026-09-23 · Impact 18 · Confirmed AU/NZ
5

CISA Warns of Heap Overflow in Orthanc DICOM Server Used for Medical Imaging

CISA has published ICS Medical Advisory ICSMA-26-253-02 covering CVE-2026-87020, an integer overflow in Orthanc's pitch and buffer-size computation that leads to a heap out-of-bounds write when the server decodes an attacker-supplied PNG or JPEG image, resulting in a crash of the Orthanc process and a denial-of-service condition. The flaw is rated CVSS 8.1 and affects Orthanc DICOM Server versions below 1.13.0; exploitation requires authenticated remote access, and CISA reports no known public exploitation at the time of publication. Orthanc is an open-source DICOM server widely embedded in imaging workflows in hospitals and research settings precisely because it is lightweight and free, which means it is frequently deployed by clinical engineering teams without a formal vendor patch relationship. The advisory is one of three CISA published on 10 September covering clinical integration and imaging software — the others being ICSMA-26-253-01 for NextGen Healthcare Mirth Connect, reported in yesterday's digest, and an ICS advisory for AVEVA Pipeline Integrity Monitor — and the cluster is a useful marker of how much of healthcare's attack surface is now made up of open-source and embedded components that no single supplier tracks. Verification: Verified

Source: CISA · Tier 1/4 — Very High · 2026-09-10 · Impact 10 · Confirmed
6

Anomali Maps Ransomware Targeting Across Eight Sectors and Finds 154 of 200 Groups Hit Healthcare, Third Behind Technology and Manufacturing

Anomali's US Ransomware Industry Targeting Report analysed targeting by 200 distinct ransomware entities across eight sectors and found more than 50% observed presence in every one — technology first with 172 groups (86%), manufacturing with 166 (83%) and healthcare third with 154 (77%). The finding is not that healthcare is uniquely targeted but that it is targeted near-universally, which changes the defensive question from "will we be a target" to "which of 154 groups will arrive first". Anomali's explanation is consistent with the incident record: healthcare combines patient care, protected health information, insurance, payments and clinical operations into one extortion surface, cannot tolerate the loss of access to patient data because that is a safety risk, faces acute pressure to recover quickly, and carries legacy systems and unpatched devices that cannot be remediated on a modern cadence. The entry points named are the familiar internet-facing set — unpatched VPNs, firewalls, edge devices, backup platforms, remote monitoring and management tooling and externally reachable applications — and the recommended controls are identity-led: phishing-resistant MFA for remote access, administrators, SSO, VPN and privileged service accounts, removal of exposed RDP, stale-account review and continuous monitoring for credential exposure. Verification: Verified

1

Canada's Privacy Commissioner Opens an Investigation of IDScan Over a Breach Exposing Driver's Licence Scans

The Privacy Commissioner of Canada, Philippe Dufresne, has launched an investigation of IDScan.net, the identity-verification vendor whose cloud platform was breached in an incident reported to involve scans of 153 million people's driver's licences. The probe, announced on 21 September, will examine the company's security practices and whether its notifications to affected individuals met the requirements of Canada's federal private-sector privacy law — a second question that regulators increasingly ask separately from the breach itself, because the adequacy of notification is where most enforcement actions are ultimately grounded. IDScan told users on 4 September that hackers had obtained user data held in its cloud platform without saying how many customers were affected; the company learned of the incident on or around 1 September, hours after journalist Brian Krebs revealed the scans were for sale on the dark web. The technology is widely deployed in retail and hospitality for age and identity checks, so the affected population sits with the businesses that scanned documents as much as with the vendor, and no Australian or New Zealand equivalent action has been announced. Sweden's IMY Fines Miljödata SEK 1.8 Million for Security Failures Behind a Breach That Hit 2.2 Million People

Sweden's privacy regulator IMY imposed a SEK 1.8 million (about US$183,000) administrative fine on IT provider Miljödata for inadequate technical and organisational security under Article 32(1) GDPR, following the 25 August 2025 attack that disrupted IT services in more than 200 Swedish regions and municipalities. Miljödata supplies work-environment and HR management systems used by 80% of Sweden's municipal systems, and the leaked data included personal identity numbers, contact information, sickness absence, rehabilitation records and school incidents involving minors; the attacker demanded 1.5 Bitcoin (about US$168,000 at the time) and published the data under the name "Datacarry". IMY's specific findings are the transferable lesson for suppliers of government-facing software: the company did not perform sufficient checks when installing new software and lacked automated real-time monitoring to detect intrusions and suspicious activity, a gap that let an intrusion proceed to exfiltration unnoticed. IMY has also opened investigations into two municipalities and one region in connection with the same attack, so further penalties may follow. Verification: Verified

3

The UK's Online Safety Regulator Opens an Investigation Into Pornhub's Parent Company Over Alleged Age-Verification Failings

The UK regulator has opened an investigation into the parent company of Pornhub over alleged age-verification failings, testing the enforcement machinery created by the Online Safety Act against the largest operator in the sector. The case is being reported alongside the parallel and separate action over the site's use of Apple-powered age checks, which raises a question about whether delegating verification to a platform's own account-level signal satisfies the statutory duty or merely shifts it. The legal significance extends past one site: age assurance is now the compliance surface on which a broad set of consumer services are judged, and the regulator's findings will define what "highly effective" verification means in practice — how much certainty, what documentation, what fallback for users who do not hold a supported device account. For Australian and New Zealand platforms watching the same policy direction, the UK enforcement record is the nearest precedent for what a regulator accepts as adequate; penalties in this regime are calculated on global turnover rather than on the number of affected users. Verification: Reported

Rydox Marketplace Operator Pleads Guilty After His Brothers Were Deported

The operator of the Rydox cybercriminal marketplace pleaded guilty, following the deportation of his co-conspirator brothers, according to The Record. Rydox operated as a trading venue for stolen data and account credentials, and the case is notable less for its scale than for its enforcement path: the platform was disrupted, its principals moved between jurisdictions, and the resolution came through immigration and extradition pressure rather than a single technical takedown. The prosecution reinforces a pattern visible across this week's legal docket — the criminal ecosystem's operational layer is being reached through travel, residency and financial exposure, while the extortion and initial-access layers remain largely untouched. 5

The UK and Cambodia Sign a Scam-Centre Agreement That Includes Cyber-Forensics Training

The two governments agreed an arrangement to work against networks of illegal scam centres — operations where trafficked foreign nationals are forced to run fraud at scale against thousands of victims, including through pig butchering and fabricated romantic relationships. The agreement commits both sides to share data, coordinate action against the criminal groups, pursue prosecutions and support trafficking victims, and it adds a capability transfer: the UK will train Cambodian law enforcement in cyber investigations, digital forensics and investigative procedure. Work will also run through the UK–Interpol Global Fraud Taskforce to identify suspects, dismantle centres and disrupt the financial flows funding them. It builds on the joint UK–US sanctions against a Southeast Asian scam-centre network in October 2025, which froze millions of pounds in UK-based property, and on the UK's Fraud Strategy with its £250 million backing and new Online Crime Centre. The Fraud Minister, Lord Hanson, represented the UK at the International Conference on Combating Online Scams in Cambodia. Verification: Verified

6

A Former Army Soldier Is Sentenced to 70 Months for the AT&T and Snowflake Extortion Spree

Cameron John Wagenius, a former active-duty US Army soldier who ran a years-long cybercrime campaign from a base in Texas, was sentenced on 25 September to 70 months in prison over attacks and extortion attempts on telecom companies including AT&T. He pleaded guilty in July 2025; prosecutors said he leaked stolen call records of then-President Donald Trump as part of failed attempts to extort US$500,000 from AT&T, that he and co-conspirators including extradition defendant Connor Moucka stole billions of sensitive records and received more than US$2.5 million in combined extortion payments, and that victims — reached through the wider breach of more than 165 Snowflake customer environments — included AT&T, Ticketmaster, Advance Auto Parts and Santander. Before his December 2024 arrest, Wagenius also attempted to sell stolen data to a foreign intelligence service and sought information online about defecting to Russia, and used a self-developed credential-stealing tool called SSH Brute while on active duty. He was ordered to pay almost US$295,000 in restitution. Verification: Verified

1

China-Aligned FamousSparrow Deploys SparroWocky Backdoor Against Latin American Governments

The China-aligned state-sponsored group FamousSparrow has been observed deploying a previously unreported Windows backdoor, SparroWocky, against government agencies across Latin America since at least August 2025, according to ESET. The modular C++ backdoor — named for the "Jabberwocky" stanza found in early samples — can execute arbitrary files, act as a TCP proxy, run commands, capture screenshots, exfiltrate files and delete itself, and it integrates open-source offensive tooling (Mbed TLS, MinHook, COFF Loader, SilentMoonwalk) directly into the implant to evade analysis. Initial access is via a DLL-sideloading chain; the underlying access vector is unknown. ESET recorded targets in Argentina, Ecuador, Guatemala, Honduras, Panama, Peru, Puerto Rico and Venezuela, with roughly 90 per cent of the group's telemetry targets in the region since July 2025 — a rare, almost region-exclusive focus that ESET theorises is aimed at helping China monitor local government reactions to Trump-era US pressure, including the Panama canal-port dispute. FamousSparrow, active since at least 2019 and publicly linked to Salt Typhoon, has replaced SparrowDoor with SparroWocky as its primary implant. Verification: Verified

Methodology & Caveats

Provenance

Collection window: 1–28 September 2026 (partial-month collection, live-synced daily). Compiled 28 September 2026. 64 distinct events are reported in full, representing 68 digest entries after folding repeat coverage of the same event, with a further 6 lower-impact items listed in short form.

Collection: Built from the Cyber Digest SQLite database (cyber-digest.db) for the stated collection window, topped up by targeted web collection where a sector did not yield enough distinct events. Every entry carries a deep link to the reporting it is drawn from; entries are written from the source article, not from headline aggregation.

Ranking: Events are ranked by an impact score (0-100), not by source reliability. The score adds fixed points for confirmed data loss, records affected, operational disruption, active exploitation, criticality of the victim environment, third-party delivery, state attribution and Australian/NZ nexus. Source reliability is recorded separately as a tier and never raises a score. The practical effect is that a confirmed breach of any scale outranks an advisory with no known victim, and a well-sourced advisory cannot top a sector on the strength of its publisher alone.

Evidence grading: Each entry carries one evidence grade: Confirmed (victim, regulator or vendor has stated the facts), Probable (reported breach with unresolved scope or attribution), Reported (single-source research, vendor or press reporting), Claimed (an extortion or hacktivist group's assertion, unverified), Thwarted (an official statement that an attack was blocked before compromise). Where only a claimed figure exists, the claim is used and the entry says so.

How this is produced: Cyber Digest is produced by an automated collection and drafting pipeline with editorial review. Sources are gathered and summarised by software, then checked, scored and edited before publication. Every entry links to its original source so claims can be checked independently; nothing in this report should be relied on without that check.

Limitations

  • The incident set is bounded by what the collection window surfaced and by the capacity of each sector section. It is a curated sample of notable events, not a census; absence of an incident here is not evidence it did not occur.
  • Victim counts are usually the figures reported at the time of writing and change as investigations complete. Claimed record counts are marked as claims and should not be read as verified.
  • Attribution is stated at the confidence the source supports. Where reporting offers a state nexus at medium confidence, the entry says so and the actor roll-up carries the same caveat rather than upgrading it.
  • Collection is weighted to English-language, publicly reported incidents, which over-represents US regulatory activity and under-represents incidents in jurisdictions with weaker disclosure obligations.
  • Australian and New Zealand relevance is flagged per event; where it is asserted in a sector narrative it refers to that flagged nexus, not to a general applicability claim.
  • 42 of the 64 events reported in full are graded Confirmed; the remainder are probable, reported or claimed and are labelled as such.
