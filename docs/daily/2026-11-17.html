Executive Summary

Australia's second-largest retailer Coles is investigating a cyber security incident after confirming an intrusion over the weekend, while in France a September attack on La Poste's mail platform Maileva has been publicly confirmed to have exposed the names and addresses of 22 million people — roughly one in three residents — and in the United States ticket seller Fandango has confirmed a card-skimmer compromise on its checkout page. Microsoft, meanwhile, has blamed a software bug introduced in July for last week's near-24-hour Microsoft 365 outage that left more than 80% of its networking gateway servers unresponsive. The day's research themes are as significant as its incidents: Amazon's threat-intelligence team has documented AI agents autonomously hunting exposed AI applications for provider API keys, Dutch consultancy Midnight Blue has found six unpatched flaws in Danfoss refrigeration controllers that allow remote takeover without login, and telecom-security firm Enea has detailed two threat groups — including the newly identified SilentAir — operating inside mobile networks for subscriber interception. On the statecraft side, King Charles III opened UK's National Cyber Force headquarters, a House of Lords committee warned the British government is "complacent" and "underprepared" for cyberattacks from Russia, China and Iran, and the EU sanctioned the pro-Russian hacktivist group NoName057(16) alongside the Doppelgänger disinformation contractor.

Coles Group's confirmation that it is working with the Australian Cyber Security Centre and global experts on a "cyber security incident" is the first major Australian corporate victim disclosure of the new week, and the country's most significant retail-sector incident since the 2023 Latitude breach. The company says it has found no evidence customer, team or supplier data was compromised or removed, that its 1,800-plus stores, Flybuys, and online channels are unaffected, and that the only visible symptom so far is a small number of Liquorland click-and-collect issues — leaving open the question of whether the intrusion matches the pattern of recent retail-sector attacks against the likes of Ahold Delhaize and TFG London. No data-theft evidence means no mandatory Notifiable Data Breaches report is due yet; that obligation to the OAIC would crystallise if the investigation concludes personal information was compromised. The Microsoft 365 outage's root cause matters locally because the near-24-hour disruption hit Australian businesses mid-week, drove a phishing wave exploiting the outage (iTnews, 14 November), and its "weaker test hardware masked the bug" explanation is a caution for AU organisations' own change management. ACSC has made no new publication since its 12 November AI-testing-environment guidance (covered 13 November) — the AI-security focus of that guidance maps directly onto today's Amazon AI-agent campaign. No new OAIC NDB notification has been published since 13 November.

The week to 16 November (11–16 November) produced 69 stories in the digest corpus, led by zero-days and vulnerabilities (19), breaches and data leaks (13) and ransomware (12), with the United States the primary geographic locus (36 stories), followed by Global (14), Europe (8), Australia (6) and APAC (5). Three structural themes carry through. First, AI-enabled intrusion tooling is now routine rather than novel: this week moved from the FBI's Artex flash alert (14 November), to Friday's Cursor/Fireworks marketplace compromise, to Amazon's discovery of AI agents autonomously harvesting API keys from exposed AI applications (16 November) — and the Fireworks fallout has widened to named victims Uber (11,940 driver records) and Notion (chat transcripts), per state-filing data. Second, state offensive cyber is being legitimised and contested in parallel: Germany's postwar intelligence reform (13 November), the NCF headquarters opening and the Lords committee's "stop being squeamish" warning (both 16 November) sit alongside the EU's sanctions on NoName057(16) and Moscow's denial of the Romanian email interception attributed to FSB-linked Turla. Third, the telecom signalling plane is an active espionage battleground: Enea's SilentAir/LIMINAL PANDA research (16 November) shows SS7, Diameter and now GTP-U in 5G networks under sustained abuse for subscriber interception, 2FA defeat and state targeting. The week ahead: the UK's Cyber Security and Resilience Bill faces its key vote within days; the Check Point PHP2Shell exploitation's full scope remains unresolved; and organisations using exposed AI gateways (LiteLLM, Dify, One API) should treat API-key hygiene as urgent.

1

Coles Group investigating a cyber security incident, no evidence of data theft so far

Coles Group Limited, Australia's second-largest retailer with more than 1,800 outlets and a Flybuys loyalty programme of over nine million active members, confirmed on 16 November that it is investigating a "cyber security incident". In a statement, the company said it has not identified any evidence that customer, team or supplier data has been compromised or removed from its systems, that it is working with the Australian Cyber Security Centre and global cyber security experts to contain and secure its IT environment, and that stores, online channels, Flybuys, Coles Plus and subscription services have not been affected. The only visible impact flagged was a small number of Liquorland click-and-collect orders. Coles has not said when the incident was discovered, whether a ransom was demanded, or whether any group has claimed responsibility. The incident follows a pattern of recent retail-sector intrusions, and leaves Australian shoppers exposed to a heightened risk of targeted phishing and scams; Coles has advised customers to use strong, unique passwords and to remain vigilant against suspicious messages soliciting personal information.

2

Fandango confirms payment-card skimmer on checkout pages

US movie-ticket platform Fandango Media — which sells tickets for around 31,000 cinema screens including AMC, Regal and Cinemark, and operates Rotten Tomatoes — has notified customers that a card-skimmer injected into its website checkout page intercepted payment details between 8 and 11 October. The company determined on 30 October that personal information may have been impacted, and says exposed data potentially includes payment card number, CVV, expiry date, billing postcode and email address, but not names, government-issued identity information or Social Security numbers. Fandango says it has fixed the issue, found no evidence of fraud using the stolen data, and is offering two years of free credit and dark-web monitoring. It has not disclosed the number of affected customers. The compromise underscores the continuing threat of Magecart-style web skimming to e-commerce and ticketing platforms, and the window in which card data intercepted at checkout can be monetised before issuers respond.

La Poste's Maileva confirms September attack exposed 22 million people's names and addresses

Maileva, the business mail platform run by France's La Poste group through its digital arm Docaposte, has confirmed that a September cyberattack exposed the names and postal addresses of approximately 22 million people — roughly one in three residents of France and one of the country's largest data breaches. Confirmed publicly via AFP on 14 November, the attack is believed to have begun on 9 September, been detected on 16–17 September, and forced a six-day service shutdown from 23 September; the attacker exploited an application flaw to reach production systems and a mail-tracking database holding 39 million letter references, from which 22 million distinct recipients were identified. Maileva notified the CNIL, filed a criminal complaint, and the Paris prosecutor's cybercrime section (BL2C) has opened an investigation. Some clients report letter contents were readable, including hospital payment reminders from Hôpital Foch and invoices from housing providers. No bank details are involved and no group has claimed the attack. Because GDPR notification obligations fall on Maileva's client companies, many of the 22 million may never receive direct notice.

1

Italian court places Revolut under judicial administration over AML control failures

An Italian court has placed the European business of Revolut — the Lithuania-based Revolut Bank UAB, operating in Italy through a local branch — under judicial administration for one year over "serious deficiencies" in its anti-money-laundering controls. The court found lax controls enabled 24 people, some under investigation for suspected mafia links or already convicted of crimes, to conceal financial flows totalling €41.9 million between 2021 and 2026, according to a court document reported by Reuters. Two judicial administrators will oversee the firm with inspection powers, and Revolut must execute a 15-point remediation plan, appoint an internal liaison officer within five days and submit an action plan within 60 days. Revolut is not itself under investigation and says it will cooperate fully. The action follows an €11.5 million Italian competition fine earlier this year and a €3.5 million Lithuanian central-bank fine last year for AML failings, and signals intensifying scrutiny of rapid-scale fintech compliance programmes across Europe.

1

Amazon uncovers AI-agent campaign harvesting API keys from exposed AI applications

Amazon's threat-intelligence team has documented attackers' AI agents autonomously hunting for weaknesses in internet-exposed AI applications — including LLM gateways and proxies such as LiteLLM, One API, New API, Veloera and Sub2API, and app builders such as Dify, FastGPT, Langflow, RAGFlow, Open WebUI, LobeChat and Ollama — to steal the AI provider keys stored inside them. Using a decoy network called MadPot that posed as popular LLM services from 3 October, Amazon captured the agents' own records, which describe stolen keys being used to run the very models powering the hunt for more keys. The team assessed with moderate confidence that the agents ran on ARTEX, the open-source AI penetration-testing tool named in the FBI's flash alert last week, and that operators were separate China-linked groups. The records include a playbook of default admin credentials (such as LiteLLM's default master key), an authentication bypass via the `isCliRequest` header in One API-family systems, and SSRF through URL-fetch features targeting cloud metadata services. Cloudflare separately reported ARTEX using a compromised CI/CD token to make 400 database-dump requests in an hour. Amazon notified affected AWS customers.

2

Microsoft says months-old software bug caused global Microsoft 365 outage

Microsoft has blamed a software bug introduced in July for last week's near-24-hour global Microsoft 365 outage, which began at 11:20 AM ET on 12 November and left more than 80% of its networking gateway servers unresponsive at its lowest point. In a preliminary post-incident report, the company said the update — deployed for more than three months without issues — contained a latent defect that could overwhelm servers under specific traffic conditions. The cascade was triggered after a Microsoft Teams failover increased traffic on Exchange Online's infrastructure roughly 15 hours before the outage, compounded by nearly 14% of gateway capacity already being offline for an unrelated reason. Microsoft acknowledged its load testing used weaker hardware with fewer cores than production machines and so missed the "contention issue", and that safeguards designed to isolate regional failures did not contain the spread. Recovery was difficult because restarted servers were quickly re-overwhelmed by backed-up traffic. A final post-incident report is expected later this week. The outage disrupted Outlook, Teams, Exchange Online and Copilot worldwide and was exploited by phishers within hours.

3

NHN Dooray confirms breach after password-reset brute force and leaked API keys

South Korean cloud collaboration provider NHN Dooray has confirmed a data breach at its Dooray! suite, used by Korean companies, government agencies and public institutions, after an attacker took over customer accounts overnight on 12–13 November and stole business files and employee contact details. The company identified two distinct weaknesses: brute-forcing the "find password" verification process until a code passed, and a separate Open API flaw that exposed API keys the attacker used for their own calls. Stolen material includes work content from projects, drives, messenger and calendars, plus employee details such as user IDs, names, email addresses, phone numbers, departments, ranks and company names. Dental-implant maker Osstem Implant confirmed 4,125 accounts across its offices were affected. NHN Dooray reported the intrusion to KISA on 13 November and the PIPC on 14 November, revoked all API and admin keys, suspended API access pending a security review, and has not disclosed the full count of affected companies — including whether any of its roughly 150 public-sector users or the 300,000-person military collaboration platform Gukbang I-eum were impacted.

US healthcare providers LifeTouch, MPOWERHealth and Geisinger disclose breaches

Three US healthcare organisations announced data breaches through first-party notices and regulator filings on 16 November. Arkansas hospice operator LifeTouch Health (formerly Arkansas Hospice) reported a March 2026 network intrusion to the HHS Office for Civil Rights affecting 38,629 individuals, with potentially compromised data including names, dates of birth, diagnoses, treatment information and Social Security numbers. Texas-based MPOWERHealth Holdings notified the California Attorney General of an incident identified around 26 June 2025, with protected health information confirmed exposed on 20 October 2026 — potentially including names, dates of birth, SSNs, driver's licence numbers, financial account information, medical and health insurance information — and began mailing notification letters on 13 November; the affected count has not been disclosed. Pennsylvania healthcare system Geisinger reported an employee email account compromise identified on 20 August 2026 affecting fewer than 2,000 individuals, with patient names, dates of birth, medical record numbers and insurance carrier names exposed, but no medical or financial information. The cluster reinforces the persistent targeting of US healthcare organisations and the multi-month gap between intrusion and public disclosure.

1

FBI warns Gootloader uses SEO poisoning and compromised WordPress sites to breach networks

The FBI has issued a FLASH alert (CC-111626-MW, 16 November, coordinated with CISA and released TLP:CLEAR) warning that Gootloader — an initial-access framework built on a JavaScript loader, compromised websites and search-engine-optimisation techniques — remains active as a ransomware precursor, linked to more than US$28 million in ransomware payments. As of October 2026 the FBI had identified more than 1,000 infected WordPress sites and 216 domains controlled by the Gootloader administrator. The 2025–2026 campaigns target US legal professionals in particular: operators poison search results for low-competition queries such as jurisdiction-specific contract clauses, luring lawyers and litigation teams to fake answer pages that deliver a malicious archive. The alert details the WordPress abuse chain (brute-forcing `xmlrpc.php`, exploiting vulnerable plugins, injecting posts and modifying JavaScript), evasion techniques including AMSI patching and a UAC bypass, and post-compromise activity spanning Kerberoasting, credential dumping, pass-the-hash and lateral movement. The FLASH lands days after Operation Endgame took down Gootloader infrastructure, and does not name the ransomware groups behind the payments. BreachNews — Tier 2 — 2026-11-16.

1

King Charles opens new National Cyber Force headquarters as UK peers warn of complacency

King Charles III opened Ribble House, the new headquarters of the UK's National Cyber Force (NCF), near the Lancashire village of Samlesbury on 16 November. The secretive unit — which combines staff from GCHQ, the Ministry of Defence, MI6 and the Defence Science and Technology Laboratory — conducts offensive cyber operations against hostile states, terrorists and criminals, and officials say it carries out operations every day. The UK government has committed £5 billion (US$6.5 billion) to the NCF through 2030 as part of a wider post-REDSPICE-style build-out of state offensive capability. The opening coincided with a House of Lords National Resilience Committee report warning the British government appears "complacent" and is "underprepared" for "potentially catastrophic risks" including cyberattacks from Russia, China and Iran, and that its forthcoming Cyber Security and Resilience Bill — due for a key vote within days — is limited in scope and does not set the security standards needed. The report urges ministers to widen cyber regulation and give regulators sufficient resources.

2

BO Team exploits Log4Shell in VMware vCenter to compromise Russian firm's code-signing server

An investigation by Kaspersky's ICS CERT has documented a months-long intrusion into an unnamed Russian software developer's network, attributed to a group tracked as BO Team (aka Black Owl, Hoody Hyena, Lifting Zmiy) and assessed to be of Ukrainian origin. The attackers gained initial access through an exposed SSH service on a Linux terminal server, moved across the network, and successfully compromised a VMware vCenter server (version 7.0.3) vulnerable to CVE-2021-44228 — Log4Shell — obtaining a remote root command line, connecting to the hypervisor over SSH and planting a persistent backdoor in `/etc/cron.hourly/` disguised as a PostgreSQL component that downloads and runs the GSocket remote-control utility. The intrusion compromised all components of the organisation's IT infrastructure, including CI/CD systems and a software code-signing server, which Kaspersky says indicated the attack was likely developing into a supply-chain scenario. Active since at least 2023, BO Team focuses on degrading Russia's military-industrial complex, using tools including Mythic, Cobalt Strike, BrockenDoor and ZeronetKit. The case illustrates 2021-era vulnerabilities still yielding root-level compromise today.

EU sanctions pro-Russia hacktivist group NoName057(16) and Doppelgänger disinformation contractor

The European Union has sanctioned the pro-Russian hacktivist group NoName057(16) — known for distributed denial-of-service attacks against critical infrastructure and election candidates across Europe — alongside 31 individuals and entities tied to Russian destabilisation activity, including the Social Design Agency (SDA) behind the Doppelgänger disinformation campaign and 24 of its staff. The decision, published 16 November, adds SDA employees to a regime targeting operations against the EU itself, giving Brussels additional tools including bans on transactions involving physical infrastructure such as servers and network equipment. The European Council formally alleged SDA's foreign-interference operations require sign-off from senior Kremlin figure Sergei Kiriyenko. The sanctions follow US charges against an alleged NoName057(16) member in September and recent research tying the group to the publication of European officials' personal data. The action extends the pattern of coordinated Five Eyes and EU responses to Russian hybrid operations, including cyber attacks, and signals continued pressure on the infrastructure that sustains state-aligned hacktivism.

Six unpatched Danfoss refrigeration controller flaws could allow remote takeover without login

Dutch security consultancy Midnight Blue has disclosed six vulnerabilities in Danfoss AK-SM800A refrigeration controllers — the equipment that runs commercial fridges and freezers — that could allow a remote attacker to take over a controller without logging in. No patch is available: the researchers say the newest firmware, version 5.4.0, does not fix the flaws. Five of the bugs sit in web request handlers that require no login and run as root, and the sixth is in the gateway that enforces Danfoss's "Strict mode" login protection, meaning the safeguard cannot be relied on. Cybersecurity agencies in Belgium, Ireland and Germany have told operators to take the controllers' management interfaces off the internet; Germany's BSI initially said 5.4.0 was unaffected and later corrected that it cannot rule out impact. The controllers were targeted in attacks on French supermarkets from 11 October and in a second wave across Europe last week that caused unwanted defrost cycles and destroyed stock, though agencies say there is no evidence linking the two. No CVE identifiers have been assigned and technical details are being withheld while disclosure with Danfoss proceeds.

Global (Macro) 2 stories

1

Enea details SilentAir and LIMINAL PANDA telecom-network espionage targeting subscribers

Telecom security firm Enea has published research on two threat actors operating inside mobile networks to spy on subscribers, intercept calls and text messages, and steal information — extending coverage of the previously reported SilentAir campaign with new detail on both groups. Enea identified SilentAir as a newly documented actor that has compromised mobile operators since before 2025, launching SS7 and Diameter attacks from compromised network nodes in at least six Asian countries, with Taiwan the main target including political and government figures; interception activity dominates its operations at 18.3% of attacks compared to around 2% for commercial surveillance firms. The researchers also documented ongoing 2026 activity by LIMINAL PANDA, including a new version of the GTPDoor malware (v3) that hides in GTP-U, the protocol also used in 5G networks, making it harder to detect. Enea directly saw or strongly suspects 5–10 infected operators worldwide, and links between the two groups are possible but unconfirmed. The report warns the IPX interconnect between operators must be treated as untrusted and recommends GTP-C and GTP-U firewalls that share intelligence with SS7 and Diameter security tools.

2

Russia denies intercepting Romanian government emails attributed to Turla

Russia has denied accusations that its state-backed hackers targeted a Romanian government institution in an attempt to intercept emails exchanged with NATO and the European Union. The Russian Embassy in Bucharest dismissed the allegations as "yet another fabrication by the Romanian intelligence services" and part of a narrative about "mythical 'hybrid attacks'", saying Bucharest had provided no concrete evidence. The denial follows Romania's domestic intelligence agency (SRI) saying it detected and stopped a cyberattack attributed to Turla, the long-running Russian espionage group linked to the FSB, which infiltrated an unidentified government institution in December 2025 by exploiting vulnerabilities in Microsoft software. The disclosure followed ESET research published this month uncovering a previously unknown hacking toolkit used by Turla to compromise government email servers in Romania and Kazakhstan, with malware specifically configured to collect emails exchanged with NATO and EU institutions. Romanian investigators found no evidence sensitive information was stolen. The exchange underscores the continuing attribution-and-denial cycle around Russian espionage operations against NATO-aligned states.

Key to this page

Two pill families appear in the text and they answer different questions. A CVE pill colours severity — a measured CVSS band from the National Vulnerability Database. A threat-actor pill colours attribution confidence — how well-corroborated the naming is, which is a claim rather than a measurement. Both are links: a CVE opens the ATT&CK matrix or its wiki page, an actor opens its wiki page.
