Executive Summary

1Password disclosed that an attacker took control of an employee's corporate GitHub account, pushed malicious code into its backend services and used it to pull customer account records, while also walking off with internal source code and access to its CI/CD environment; password vaults and the keys protecting them were untouched, but the stolen material includes provisioning and recovery tokens and a list of verified customers that is now ideal phishing bait. Australia's Flight Centre Travel Group confirmed a breach of the third-party accommodation booking system shared by its leisure brands, with passport details, dates of birth and in rare cases health or mobility information among the records potentially exposed. In the same 48 hours, Accenture's APEX Labs showed that five unauthenticated Mitel MiCollab flaws, one of them a CVSS 10.0 command injection, were exploited before Mitel's patch existed, with the attacker reaching an Active Directory domain controller 32 minutes after its first request; CISA added all five to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue with a 28 November federal deadline. And a North Korea-linked worm was found to have poisoned 7,609 GitHub repositories by forging commits under their owners' names, reading its command-and-control address from an Ethereum wallet so that takedowns achieve nothing.

Flight Centre is the day's direct Australian exposure, and the shape of it is a supplier problem rather than a perimeter failure: the affected platform is a third-party accommodation booking system shared by the Flight Centre brand, Travel Associates, Cruiseabout and some independent agents, while flight bookings and corporate travel sit on separate systems and are unaffected. The company has not put a number on the records taken, saying only that most held names, some basic travel details, and a very small number passport details, email addresses, phone numbers and dates of birth, with health or mobility information in rare cases; it found no evidence that card, payment or bank details were accessed, has engaged Mandiant alongside the supplier, is rotating authentication keys and passwords, and says it is notifying the relevant authorities. For an ASX-listed travel group the operative question is the Privacy Act's Notifiable Data Breaches scheme: once the assessment is complete, an eligible breach where serious harm is likely must be reported to the OAIC and to affected individuals, and passport plus date-of-birth combinations are exactly the identity-verification material that makes serious harm arguable. The Mitel and Danfoss entries matter locally on the same logic — both are internet-facing or OT products with Australian enterprise installs, and CISA's deadlines carry no local force, so the patch clock here is set by exposure rather than by a regulator. The ACSC published nothing new: all three listings were re-checked today and the newest item remains the 17 November alert on vulnerability cadence, with no in-place update to any earlier advisory.

New Zealand's National Cyber Security Centre published its Quarter Three Cyber Security Insights report on 23 November, and the numbers are the sharpest signal out of Wellington this quarter: 1,694 incident reports between July and September, up from 1,129 in Q2 and the highest quarterly total since Q3 2024, with 179 assessed as requiring specialist technical support against 92 the quarter before. Direct financial losses reported to the NCSC climbed to $5.9 million, a 118 per cent increase on the previous quarter's $2.7 million. Phishing and credential harvesting remained the most reported category at 511 incidents, up 56 per cent, and reports involving command-and-control server hosting hit 94, the highest since Q3 2025 — a figure that measures networks already compromised rather than attempts blocked. Education and Training reports rose from 13 to 43, a 230 per cent jump. Deputy Director-General Catriona Robinson tied the rise to frontier AI systems accelerating both discovery and exploitation of vulnerabilities, the same judgement Switzerland's NCSC reached from its own agent-driven intrusions this week.

Over the week to 26 November the corpus logged 90 stories across seven editions. Vulnerability and zero-day coverage led with 29, breach and data leak followed on 28 and ransomware on 19; the United States dominated geography with 50, then Global and Australia tied on 13, Europe on 8 and APAC on 6; IT and Technology took 24 of the 90 stories, Government 15 and Healthcare 8. Three threads run through it. First, exploitation now outruns patch availability rather than lagging it: Mitel's five MiCollab flaws were worked before the 16 November advisory became a patch, Accenture's timeline puts root at 30 minutes and a domain controller at 32, and CISA's catalogue absorbed 10 new entries on 25 November and three more the day before — Drupal, Idunno AntiCsrf and Optimizely among them. Second, identity and developer tooling has become the preferred way in. 1Password lost an employee GitHub account; Todoist lost a version-control credential that was then used to rewrite three repositories' automation workflows and escalate to an administrative credential; PolinRider forges commits under real developers' names and hides its loader in build configuration. The common control is not a firewall but the trust placed in a commit, a token or a workflow. Third, the AI-assisted tempo is being confirmed by national agencies rather than vendors: Switzerland said every agent-driven attack it knew of succeeded, New Zealand's NCSC attributes its record quarter partly to frontier-model acceleration, and Check Point's Volant Manticore operation shows an Iranian group building IPFS-routed tooling and testing it against antivirus before deployment. For the week ahead, the exposure to review is the one that authenticates rather than the one that serves: MiCollab and Juniper Session Smart appliances, unpatched Danfoss refrigeration controllers sitting on flat store networks, and any CI/CD pipeline whose credentials live in a developer's personal account.

IT / Technology 5 stories

1

1Password says an attacker took an employee's GitHub account and used it to steal customer records and source code

1Password disclosed on 25 November that an attacker compromised an employee's corporate GitHub account and used it to reach private source code repositories and inject malicious code into backend services, which then downloaded customer account records. The stolen data includes customer names and email addresses, account and user IDs, account creation and last sign-in timestamps, public cryptographic keys, Secure Remote Password parameters, invitation, provisioning and account recovery tokens, and other internal metadata; internal source code and access to the CI/CD environment went too, potentially exposing internal infrastructure credentials. Password vaults and the encryption keys protecting them were not compromised, and the tokens cannot be used on their own because completing an invitation or recovery requires single sign-on or a six-digit code sent to the registered address — codes have since been strengthened to ten alphanumeric characters as an anti-brute-force measure. The company disabled the account, revoked the stolen token, restored clean services, rotated exposed infrastructure credentials and engaged external validation, and says it found no evidence of customer account takeovers.

2

A North Korea-linked worm forged developers' commits to poison 7,609 GitHub repositories, and it comes back after cleanup

Apiiro reported that a North Korea-linked worm it calls PolinRider had spread through at least 7,609 GitHub repositories owned by 2,330 accounts, most belonging to working developers, with victims including Neutralinojs, InstaWP Connect and fxhash. Between 27 October and 6 November it pushed its loader into 2,040 repositories, 956 of them newly infected. The mechanism is the notable part: using stolen GitHub access, it rewrites commits the developer already made while preserving author, message and date, and appends a loader hidden after hundreds of spaces in a build configuration file, so building the project runs it — and in the newest wave, opening the folder in VS Code is enough. The loader reads its command-and-control address from an Ethereum wallet, so taking a server down changes nothing because a single transaction moves every loader to the next. Cleanup does not hold: 1,949 repositories were reinfected after being publicly listed, and nine of sixteen repositories carrying a "remove PolinRider" commit had the loader pushed back over the cleanup. Two infected repositories shipped the loader to npm through their own release pipelines, and the chain terminates in BeaverTail and InvisibleFerret, North Korea's usual stealers.

3

Preinstalled malware shipped in firmware on more than 220,000 Android devices this year, and it cannot be removed

Kaspersky reported that Keenadu, a firmware-level Android backdoor first documented in February, landed on more than 220,000 devices in the first ten months of 2026, and that Keenadu and the older Triada family accounted for 88 per cent of devices found with preinstalled malware this year; detections have grown more than 2.5 times year on year and about twenty distinct Triada variants appeared in 2026. The Keenadu samples examined stayed dormant for roughly two and a half months before their command-and-control servers sent an activation order, which the researchers read as deliberate detection avoidance. Both families are modular, letting operators push additional payloads and inject functionality into any app on the device, and in most known Keenadu cases the objective was ad fraud through generated clicks. Because the malware sits in the system partition, Kaspersky says removal by antivirus or the operating system is technically impossible and some variants survive reflashing. It arrives through the supply chain — installed deliberately, supplied by a bad-faith contractor, or introduced through a compromised build component — with related families found in children's smartwatch and TV box firmware.

4

A 12-year-old Linux kernel NFS flaw gives unauthenticated remote root to anyone who can reach port 2049

OpenSec Intelligence published research on NFS2ROOT, tracked as CVE-2026-99362 at a reported CVSS score of 9.8, a pre-authentication remote root flaw in the kernel's NFS server present since Linux 3.16 in 2014. The defect sits in the NFSv4.1 session reply cache: a client may negotiate the cache to its minimum size, and after the reply buffer is narrowed a COMPOUND tag can fill it far enough that the encoder stops without recording an error, the operation is treated as a success and the copy overruns a zero-length kernel heap buffer. The included KASAN report shows an 80-byte out-of-bounds write, 61 bytes past the end of the heap object, with the client controlling most of the overflowed data; a single unauthenticated TCP connection to port 2049 on a host serving NFSv4.1 or 4.2 is sufficient. The kernel CVE team announced the fix on 19 November in 6.12.117, 6.18.61, 7.3.2 and 7.4-rc1, with backports in 5.10.275, 5.15.226, 6.1.193 and 6.6.163. OpenSec says its working root-shell exploit remains private.

5

Todoist says a stolen developer credential led to production databases and the password hashes of about 6 per cent of users

Todoist confirmed that an attacker reached its production systems on 23 November and downloaded account records for roughly 6 per cent of users, including email addresses, account creation dates and older salted SHA1 password hashes belonging to early accounts whose passwords had not changed since the company stopped using that method more than a decade ago. Smaller groups also lost API tokens, Slack integration tokens, credential-like task content and connected Outlook email details. The intrusion began on 22 November with a stolen credential tied to the company's version-control system; the attacker modified automated workflows in three repositories to collect further credentials and could read source code, then obtained an administrative credential for the employee sign-in system through an infrastructure automation workflow, changed an employee's authentication settings, signed in to cloud infrastructure as that employee, created extra accounts and reached production databases. Todoist says it found no changes to application code and has not established how the original credential was stolen, with a compromised employee device as the leading but unconfirmed theory.

Australia's Flight Centre says a shared booking platform exposed customer records, including passport details for a small number

Flight Centre Travel Group disclosed on 26 November that unusual activity in a third-party accommodation booking system may have exposed a significant number of customer records across its leisure brands. The platform is shared by the Flight Centre brand, Travel Associates, Cruiseabout and some independent agents; flight bookings and corporate travel, which run on separate systems, were not affected. The company has not determined how many records were extracted, but its initial review found most contained only customer names and some basic travel information. A very small number held passport details, email addresses, phone numbers and dates of birth, and in rare cases records may have included health or mobility information — sensitive when linked to a travel itinerary. Flight Centre found no evidence that credit card, payment or bank account details were accessed. It has activated its incident response plan and is investigating with the supplier and Mandiant, is changing authentication keys and passwords, increasing monitoring, and says it is notifying the relevant authorities; existing hotel bookings proceed as planned.

FIFA confirms internal data was stolen after a new extortion group published what it claims is 12GB from its Microsoft environment

FIFA confirmed that hackers breached its systems and gained "unauthorised access to a limited set of personal and business information", saying it is working with law enforcement and condemning "the unlawful acquisition and publication of information, particularly personal data". The governing body, which has about 700 employees and is headquartered in Zurich, did not answer follow-up questions on the nature of the incident. The Handsome Boy Hacking Club, a group that emerged only the previous week, claimed it stole more than 12GB, attempted to extort FIFA and failed, then published the material on a cybercriminal forum, saying it had accessed FIFA's Microsoft environment; the alleged haul includes identity and immigration documents, financial records, legal documents and governance information. None of that claim has been independently verified. The incident follows an attack on Spain's LaLiga, which said on Monday that hackers stole personal information belonging to fans, and the arrest of a suspect over a breach at Ajax in May.

3

A CVSS 9.8 flaw in supermarket refrigeration controllers has no patch, after attackers spent the year turning store cooling off

CISA warned about CVE-2026-129713, a stack-based buffer overflow rated 9.8 in the Danfoss AK-SM 8xxA series — the central controllers supermarkets and convenience stores use to run refrigerated display cases, compressor racks, temperature logging, and in some models store lighting and HVAC. An unauthenticated attacker can trigger the flaw to restart the server and potentially achieve remote code execution with the web server's privileges. The 820A, 850A and 880A models are deployed worldwide in numbers CISA says remain unclear. No patch exists: Danfoss's advisory of 20 November says the fix lands in software version 5.5.x, which will also remove the option to turn off "Strict mode". The warning follows a year of attacks on European supermarkets and food retailers in which hackers remotely shut down cooling systems or raised their temperatures. CISA's guidance is exposure reduction rather than remediation — keep controllers off the public internet, segregate control systems from business networks behind firewalls, and require a current VPN for remote access.

1

Five Mitel MiCollab zero-days were exploited before the patch shipped, and one took an attacker from first request to domain controller in 32 minutes

Accenture's APEX Labs reported that attackers exploited critical Mitel MiCollab flaws before Mitel released a patch, reaching Active Directory domain controllers and stealing database contents in what it assesses as a data-theft extortion campaign. All five bugs sit in the Audio, Web and Video Conferencing component of MiCollab 10.3 (10.3.0.18) and earlier: CVE-2026-133323 is blind command injection at CVSS 10.0, CVE-2026-133303 XML injection leading to command execution at 9.0, and CVE-2026-133311, CVE-2026-133275 and CVE-2026-133324 are weaker authentication, upload and bypass flaws at 5.9 and 5.3. Mitel disclosed them on 16 November and added CVE numbers on 24 November. At the confirmed victim, web shell activity began before 23:00 UTC on 15 November; authentication was bypassed a minute later, the first web shell command ran 12 minutes after the first request, root came within 30 minutes, and at 32 minutes the attacker used the directory credentials MiCollab stores for its LDAP connector to sign in to a domain controller. It exported directory records, ran Kerberoasting, pulled plaintext credentials from file shares and about 20 hours in dumped databases from backup and line-of-business servers, exfiltrating them in hundreds of short HTTPS sessions and filtering its own requests out of the server logs. CISA added all five to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 25 November in a 10-entry batch that also covered Juniper Session Smart, Vates Xen Orchestra and Danfoss, with federal agencies required to fix four by 28 November and the fifth by 9 December.

2

Britain's National Cyber Action Plan slips again, to 9 December, as a Lords committee calls the government complacent

The UK government has again delayed its National Cyber Action Plan, the strategy for defending the wider economy against state-backed and criminal hackers. Conceived as a national cyber strategy and first due by the end of 2025, it was rebranded as an action plan, came close to publication in July before being postponed amid Keir Starmer's resignation, and was then expected in mid-November; officials now aim for 9 December. A government spokesperson declined to comment on timing while insisting ministers remain committed to publishing it. The delay follows a cross-party House of Lords National Resilience Committee report finding Britain dangerously underprepared for crises that cascade across the economy, with the committee warning the government "appears complacent" about "potentially catastrophic risks" including attacks from Russia, China and Iran. Baroness Northover said further absence of an overarching plan "is not just frustrating, it is serious". The pattern extends to legislation: the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill took more than four years to reach Parliament and is not expected to be enforced until 2028, a decade after the NIS Regulations it replaces.

Standard Chartered's crypto custody arm Zodia confirms client and staff data leaked in a breach

Zodia Custody, the cryptocurrency custody business Standard Chartered recently moved to full ownership, reported a cyber attack in which client and staff names and email addresses were leaked. Chief executive Richard Clark said the investigation had so far found "no unauthorised transactions", and that the platform had run on offline software immediately after the breach was reported, allowing users to connect directly, before being switched back online. Clark described the accessed information as "limited" client data — client records, information on Zodia itself, and contact names and email addresses for several clients and employees. The firm did not say how many people were affected or how the attacker got in. The disclosure lands in the same week as the FinCEN intrusion and a global Microsoft outage, and matters for the custody segment specifically because institutional crypto custody sells on the assurance that keys are segregated and transaction paths are controlled — a breach that reaches client identity records but not funds still puts a target list in circulation.

Keio's September ransomware attack may have exposed data on 10.63 million customers and partners, including 1.6 million passport copies

Keio Corporation, the Tokyo operator behind the Keio Line railway, Keio Plaza Hotel and Keio department store, said the ransomware attack it detected on 26 September 2026 may have exposed personal information on about 10.63 million customers and business partners, a figure it disclosed on 24 November alongside its final report to Japan's Personal Information Protection Commission. The possible exposure also covers about 360,000 job applicants and about 77,000 current and former employees and their dependents, with overlaps between groups so the totals cannot simply be added. Customer records that may have leaked include names, addresses, phone numbers and email addresses; for some people far more sensitive material is in scope, including about 1.6 million passport copies from overseas guests at four hotels, about 20,000 department-store card numbers, roughly 46,000 identity documents collected in property sales and rental contracts, and copies of six customers' My Number cards. Confirmed leaks are smaller: about 12,000 employees, former employees and dependents, about 160 job applicants and about 150 tenants. Keio says it has found no financial losses or misuse, and its Keio Presso Inn chain is still not taking new reservations.

Nearly 14,000 gaming consoles sit on healthcare production networks, 95 per cent of them outside any segmented zone

Claroty analysed more than 45 million devices across 30,000-plus critical infrastructure locations and found a consequential population of consumer equipment on production networks. In healthcare it identified 14,624 gaming consoles, and 13,939 of them — 95.3 per cent — were connected to production networks rather than guest or IoT segments, despite consoles serving a legitimate purpose in children's hospitals. Of the healthcare consoles, 691 (4.7 per cent) were rated critical or high risk because they carried known vulnerabilities with high CVSS scores, appeared in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue, had high Exploit Prediction Scoring System scores, or ran outdated, unsupported or end-of-life software. The researchers' point is that medical devices face strict pre-market security requirements while consumer devices face none, yet both share network infrastructure with industrial control systems, building management systems and clinical equipment — and hospitals typically lack visibility into what is actually connected. The finding is a segmentation failure rather than a patching one: guest and IoT network separation would have contained most of it.

1

The ICO reprimands the Home Office after its border system left people wrongly detained for want of a correction process

The UK Information Commissioner's Office reprimanded the Home Office over a major data-integrity failure in the Helios border control platform, introduced on 2 October 2024, finding infringements of Articles 5(1)(d) and 25(1) of the UK GDPR and sections 38(1) and 56(1) of Part 3 of the Data Protection Act 2018 — in substance, a failure to keep personal information accurate and up to date. The previous system had a dedicated team correcting errors in real time; Helios required inaccuracies to be reported to and corrected by the owner of the source data, but no mechanism existed to do so, and staff were told not to include personal information when filing reports, which the ICO said made correction "virtually impossible". A temporary reporting system arrived on 25 October 2025; by March 2026 border force officers had raised 6,500 incidents and around 5,000 incorrect records had been removed. The ICO says the uncorrected inaccuracies led to people being wrongly stopped at the border for additional checks or refused entry, likely causing psychological distress, and that none complained — possibly because the department never told them the real cause of their detention.

1

An undocumented Iranian group is running espionage against Cyprus, routing its backdoor through IPFS

Check Point documented a previously undocumented Iranian advanced persistent threat it calls Volant Manticore, targeting organisations in Cyprus for intelligence gathering on behalf of Iran's Ministry of Intelligence and Security. Targets so far include a Cyprus-based airline operating mainly between Cyprus and Israel, telecommunications providers, critical infrastructure organisations and a suspected government entity with military connections — a selection the researchers read as an effort to understand the island's government, infrastructure and relationships with regional partners. The firm assesses with medium confidence that Volant Manticore is an offshoot of MuddyWater, likely run by former members, and says it is among the first Iranian groups with a dedicated Cyprus focus, having expanded to Saudi healthcare organisations from September as Saudi-Houthi tensions rose. Initial access comes through tailored phishing; the investigation began with a fabricated résumé for a fictitious, AI-generated travel professional found on VirusTotal, whose "full CV" link delivered KuboShell, a Windows backdoor that uses the InterPlanetary File System for command-and-control and is therefore harder to block than a conventional server. Operators also use legitimate remote monitoring tools including N-able, NetLock and MeshCentral to blend in, and were observed testing malware against antivirus products.
