Chinese Routers Sold Worldwide Found to Contain Backdoors

Summary

Dark Reading reported on 27 August 2026 that security researchers have identified backdoors in certain Chinese-made routers sold globally. Firmware in these devices appears to enable remote access or control for operators, raising supply-chain concerns for home and small-office (SOHO) infrastructure in consumer and SME networks around the world.

Technical detail

The finding is that specific Chinese-manufactured consumer/SOHO router models contain firmware that appears to include a capability enabling remote access or control. The incident type is "reported", meaning the specific models and attribution have not been fully confirmed, but the presence of hidden remote-access capability in the firmware is the flagged risk. Scope matters: these are consumer/SOHO routers, not the bulk-power systems addressed by the White House executive order.

Significance

The disclosure lands squarely on the same day as the White House bulk-power equipment ban and amplifies the broader US Government theme that foreign-made networked equipment can carry hidden remote-access capability. A router is a trusted inflection point for a home or small office network: a backdoored device can see all transit, influence DNS resolution and act as a pivot into the LAN. For consumer and SOHO users, the practical challenge is that many such devices stop getting firmware updates, so a backdoor persists for the device's life.

AU/NZ relevance

Cheap made-in-China routers are common in Australian and New Zealand homes and small offices, so the finding is directly relevant to the AU/NZ consumer and SOHO network baseline. It reinforces the continued hardware-supply-chain tension in Western markets. Australian and New Zealand users of consumer routers should favour devices that receive sustained firmware updates, disable remote management, change default credentials, and track vendor advisories for any models flagged under this disclosure.
